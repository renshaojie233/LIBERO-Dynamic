01 / Findings

Main results

Three complementary views of dynamic manipulation.

Motion speed and feedback horizon both matter.

Across the six pretrained policies, faster target motion lowers success, but the decline differs between models. The speed sweep separates this sensitivity from performance at a single motion speed.

The horizon sweeps show that the best measured K is model- and execution-dependent. Shorter action segments provide more frequent observations, but their benefit depends on how inference and action execution are scheduled.

Click the figure to enlarge ↗

Queue continuity shapes the benefit of frequent feedback.

In synchronous execution, a smaller K also means more frequent inference pauses. This explains the widening gap from the zero-latency control at short horizons. In the asynchronous sweep, success peaks at K = 6, where steady queue underflow reaches zero; longer underflow-free horizons then reduce success in this checkpoint.

RTC changes both inference latency and queue handling, so its lower success cannot be attributed to latency alone. VLASH uses a separately trained checkpoint and is a system reference, rather than a same-checkpoint controller comparison.

Click the figure to enlarge ↗

Dynamic-data gains depend on the model and recipe.

All three architectures improve under at least one dynamic-data recipe. Mixed fine-tuning gives the strongest measured result for π0.5, while dynamic-only fine-tuning gives the strongest measured result for both Joint variants.

Under the matched 3,000-step dynamic-only recipe, the history-conditioned Joint gains more than the single-frame variant and prefers a different action horizon. These results motivate studying temporal conditioning and data composition together; they do not establish a universally best recipe. The two π0.5 recipes also use different training budgets.
